Which method to use

The five methods below do the same job, joining DNA, and they differ in what they need from you before you start.

  • Restriction cloning when the vector already has two usable sites and the insert has neither. It needs no PCR, it is the cheapest route, and the site stays in the product as a short scar.
  • Gibson assembly when the junction has to be exact, for instance a protein fusion with nothing added between the two halves. It puts the overlap on the primers, so it costs a PCR per piece and works for a handful of pieces at a time.
  • Golden Gate when there are many pieces and the order matters, or when you want a set of parts you can reuse. The four-base overhangs set the order, digest and ligation run in one tube, and every internal site of the enzyme has to go first.
  • Gateway® when one insert has to end up in several vectors. Making the entry clone is the work; after that each destination is one reaction, and nothing is cut or ligated.
  • TA or TOPO® when you only need the PCR product in a plasmid to sequence or store it. There is nothing to design, and the insert lands either way round unless the kit is directional.

Changing bases rather than joining pieces is a different job: mutagenesis by primer and inverse PCR both live in the Clone menu, and the primer design guide covers the primers either way.

Whichever route you take, read the sequencing result before you trust the clone.

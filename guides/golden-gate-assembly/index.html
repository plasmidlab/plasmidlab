Golden Gate assembly: how to choose the overhangs

In Golden Gate assembly the four bases that hold two pieces together are yours to pick. That choice decides the order the pieces go in, and how much of the reaction ends up wrong.

Why the overhang is yours to choose

Golden Gate assembly uses a Type IIS enzyme. These enzymes recognise one sequence and cut a fixed distance away from it, so the four bases left as a single-stranded overhang are not part of the recognition sequence. You choose them. Because the cut falls outside the site, the recognition sequence stays on the piece you discard, and the junction in the finished plasmid carries no scar.

BsaI is written GGTCTC(1/5): the enzyme cuts the top strand one base past the site and the bottom strand five bases past it, which leaves a four-base 5′ overhang. BsmBI (Esp3I) is CGTCTC(1/5) and BbsI is GAAGAC(2/6). Same idea, different spacing. In PlasmidLab, Analyze > Use Enzyme Set > Type IIS enzymes shows the whole class, and Golden Gate enzymes narrows it to the ones usually used for assembly.

Choosing a set of overhangs

Every junction needs its own overhang, and the set has to be unambiguous when all the pieces are in one tube:

  • No overhang may match another one, or another one's reverse complement.
  • Avoid palindromes such as AATT. They ligate to themselves.
  • Avoid pairs that differ at a single position. A single mismatch is the usual way a wrong junction forms.
  • Avoid 0% and 100% GC, so no AAAA, TTTT, GGGG or CCCC.
  • If your parts follow an assembly standard such as MoClo, reuse its published overhangs so the parts stay interchangeable.

Ligation fidelity has been measured for whole overhang sets, and NEB publishes the data and a set-checking tool. It is worth using once an assembly has more than four or five junctions.

Remove internal sites first

If a part contains the recognition sequence itself, the enzyme cuts the part during the reaction and the assembly fails. Removing those sites is called domestication: change one base inside a codon so the protein stays the same and the site is gone, or order the part already domesticated.

A worked example you can repeat: pUC19 (NCBI L09137, 2,686 bp, circular) has one BsaI site, cutting the top strand after base 1,760, and two BsmBI sites, after bases 3 and 45. So pUC19 is not ready to be a BsaI backbone as it stands. BbsI does not cut it at all. Paste the sequence into the restriction site finder, choose Circular, and look the three enzymes up.

Assemble it in PlasmidLab

Open the backbone and the parts with File > Open, then choose Clone > Golden Gate Assembly…. Add one slot per insert, set each piece's orientation, and review the product before you save it. The options the assembly uses live in Clone > Golden Gate Settings….

Before ordering anything, read the product: the sequence at every junction, the reading frame through each junction for a coding fusion, and that no Type IIS site survives in the assembled plasmid.
